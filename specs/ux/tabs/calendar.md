# Calendar [CAL]

The binding specification for the view over what is coming: dated things ahead, dates that land hard every year, and the difference between showing one and counting down to it.

> 🔒 **An agent may not edit this file.** The prohibition extends to any alteration of it, rewording, reformatting, renaming and moving included, and admits no exception for a change the agent judges harmless. Where an agent finds anything wrong, inconsistent or improvable, it records the finding in [`REVIEW.md`](../../REVIEW.md) with its reasoning and leaves the entry for a person to clear, never treating its own entry as settled. Until a person applies a change, this file is complied with as written; where it and the repository diverge, the repository is wrong.

## Specifications Register

> ⚠️ **When referencing:** always cite a row by **both** its ID and its spec statement — IDs shift, specs get removed, and ordering isn't guaranteed.

| ID | SPEC STATEMENT | SPEC RATIONALE | DESIGN RECOMMENDATION |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **CAL-001** | A date she asked to see is shown; a date she did not ask about is never pushed at her. | The instruction not to announce a hard anniversary is about a conversation she did not steer. A page she navigated to is the opposite case, and hiding a date she went looking for would be the surface deciding what she can handle — which is the one thing this record never does. The distinction is between *showing* and *pressing*. The test: every recurring date in the record appears on this view, and no part of the surface raises one unbidden. | Render the recurring dates plainly, in the same treatment as everything else on the page. It works because the decision is made once — this view shows, conversation is where gentleness lives — rather than per date. Its limit: it says nothing about other surfaces, where a date surfacing unbidden would still be an announcement. |
| **CAL-002** | A hard date is never counted down to. | A countdown is what turns a page she can close into a thing that follows her around: the number changes daily, it is legible at a glance, and it converts a date into an approaching event with a size. Showing the date respects that she asked; sizing the distance to it answers a question she did not. The cost of getting it wrong is that the view becomes something to avoid opening in the fortnight when it might have helped. The test: no recurring date on this view renders a remaining interval, in any form, including colour. | Render a recurring date as a date and a note on how she wants it handled, and nothing else. It works because the interval is never computed for these rows rather than computed and suppressed, so it cannot reappear through a styling change. Ordinary upcoming items are not recurring dates and may carry an interval — **CAL-003** governs those. |
| **CAL-003** | An ordinary dated thing ahead may say how far off it is. | The two cases pull opposite ways and the rule has to say so or the simpler one gets applied to both. An appointment on Thursday is a thing to plan around, and "in three days" is the fact she came for; an anniversary is not a thing to plan around and the same phrasing turns it into one. The test: an appointment renders its distance and a recurring hard date does not, on the same page, without the reader needing an explanation. | Compute the interval for dated items only, from a single notion of today shared by the whole surface. It works because one definition of today keeps two sections of one page from disagreeing about what is imminent. Its limit: the phrasing is a reading aid, and the date itself must remain present rather than replaced by it. |
| **CAL-004** | A date that has passed and not yet been filed is shown as passed, not removed. | An item moves onto the spine during a session, and sessions do not run on the day a thing happens, so the gap between the two is normal rather than a lapse. Removing the row in the meantime answers "did I actually go?" by deleting the question, and leaves a record that looks tidier than it is. The test: an item whose date is behind today still appears, visibly distinguished from what is ahead. | Keep passed items in their own section, marked, until the record moves them. It works because the section names the reason — not yet moved to the spine — so the state reads as pending rather than as forgotten. Its limit: nothing here bounds how long an item may sit there, which is an upkeep concern. |
| **CAL-005** | Recurring dates that are good are shown as distinctly as ones that are hard. | A calendar holding only the dates that hurt becomes evidence for a reading of her year that is not true, and it is the same failure as a record holding only bad days. It matters more here than elsewhere because this view is about what is coming: a year ahead made entirely of hard dates is a year she can see and dread. The test: both kinds of recurring date have a place on this view, and neither is folded into the other. | Keep two sections, each named for what it holds, in the record's own grouping. It works because the record already distinguishes them, so the view inherits the distinction rather than judging it. Its limit: the view cannot supply a good date the record does not hold — a thin section here is a write-side signal, not a layout one. |

> 📚 **No row above carries a source tag, and that is a value rather than an omission.** These registers govern one person's private record; where no published work genuinely addresses a row it stays bare, and reaching for a large name to decorate one is the failure mode — see **Source tags** in [`README.md`](../../README.md). The works cited elsewhere in these specs, and the boundary past which each stops applying, are in [`REFERENCES.md`](../../REFERENCES.md).

## Unwritten

- How far ahead the view looks, and whether there is a horizon past which a dated thing is not yet shown.
- Whether a recurring date's next occurrence is ever rendered as a concrete date, which is a countdown by another name.
- What the view does with a dated thing that has no day — a month, a season, "sometime after the move".
